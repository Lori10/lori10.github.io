Text Classification with SETFIT

A research study, written at Hochschule Darmstadt, on SETFIT: a prompt-free way to train a text classifier from only a handful of labeled examples. I reproduced the method, benchmarked it against standard BERT fine-tuning on three datasets, and proposed a new way to build its contrastive training pairs, which I called hard negative/positive sampling. The code, data, and full write-up are public.

01 · The Problem

Labeling data is slow and expensive, so many real classification tasks start with only a few dozen labeled examples. The usual options each have a catch:

  • Standard fine-tuning updates every parameter of a model like BERT. It needs a reasonably large dataset to work well and is costly to train.
  • In-context learning with large language models works with few examples, but its accuracy grows with model size, so it depends on models with billions of parameters and the infrastructure to run them.
  • Prompt-based methods such as pattern-exploiting training and many parameter-efficient fine-tuning setups need hand-written prompts, and results vary with how well those prompts are written.

SETFIT (Tunstall et al., 2022) avoids all three: it needs no prompts and uses a comparatively small Sentence Transformer. The questions for this study were whether its reported results hold up in my own experiments, and whether the way it builds training pairs could be improved.

02 · How SETFIT Works

SETFIT trains in two steps:

  • Step 1: contrastive fine-tuning of a Sentence Transformer. From the few labeled sentences, SETFIT generates sentence pairs labeled 1 (same class) or 0 (different class). The Sentence Transformer is fine-tuned on these pairs in a Siamese setup: both sentences go through the same network with shared weights, and a cosine-similarity loss pulls same-class pairs together and pushes different-class pairs apart.
  • Step 2: a classification head. The fine-tuned model encodes each original training sentence into one embedding. A logistic regression is trained on these embeddings and their labels.

At prediction time, the fine-tuned Sentence Transformer encodes the new sentence and the logistic regression predicts its class. 

few labeled sentences
  → generate pairs (s_i, s_j, 1) / (s_i, s_k, 0)
  → fine-tune Sentence Transformer (Siamese, cosine-similarity loss)
  → encode training sentences → train logistic regression
predict:  label = LogReg( ST(new_sentence) )

Pair generation is what makes this work with so little data. With M labeled examples there are M(M−1)/2 possible unique pairs, far more than M itself, so the contrastive step has much more to learn from than the raw dataset. Because pairs are built from class labels rather than meaning, fine-tuning turns general-purpose sentence embeddings into what I call topic embeddings: embeddings organised by the task's classes.

03 · My Contribution: Hard Negative/Positive Sampling

SETFIT's default pair generation is random: for each sentence s_i it picks a random same-class sentence as the positive and a random other-class sentence as the negative, and repeats this for N iterations. Many of these pairs are easy. A positive that is already close to s_i, or a negative that is already far away, teaches the model very little.

I replaced the random choice with a deliberate one. For each sentence s_i and each iteration:

  • Hard positive: pick the same-class sentence with the lowest cosine similarity to s_i, meaning the one the model currently thinks is least alike.
  • Hard negative: pick the other-class sentence with the highest cosine similarity to s_i, meaning the one the model most easily confuses with it.
  • No repeats: remove both chosen sentences from the candidate pool for s_i, so the next iteration picks the next-hardest pair instead of the same one again.
for each sentence s_i, for n in 1..N:
    s_j = argmin cos_sim(s_i, s)  over same-class candidates   → (s_i, s_j, 1)
    s_k = argmax cos_sim(s_i, s)  over other-class candidates  → (s_i, s_k, 0)
    remove s_j, s_k from s_i's candidates

The idea is to spend each training step where the embedding space is currently wrong. I expected it to help most with a small number of iterations N and a larger number of training examples M, since more examples means more genuinely hard pairs to find.

04 · Experimental Setup
  • Datasets: three binary classification sets from the SetFit organisation on the Hugging Face Hub: SST-2 (movie review sentiment), SentEval-CR (customer review sentiment), and ADE-corpus-v2 (whether a medical sentence reports an adverse drug event).
  • Training sizes: M = 18 and M = 50 labeled examples per class, plus a larger 800-example training set for the full-data baseline.
  • Robustness: few-shot results depend heavily on which examples are picked, so every configuration was run on 5 random training splits (seeds 10–50) and the mean accuracy is reported, following the original paper.
  • Model: all-mpnet-base-v2 as the Sentence Transformer. It was trained on over 1 billion sentence pairs, maps text to 768-dimensional vectors, and scored best on average across Sentence Transformers benchmarks.
  • Hyperparameters: cosine-similarity loss, learning rate 2e-5, batch size 16, 1 epoch, 20 pair-generation iterations. The same learning rate and epoch count were used for the BERT baseline, and no method was tuned, so no approach got an unfair advantage.

I compared four approaches:

  • Standard fine-tuning of bert-base-uncased on the same few-shot data, and on the 800-example training set.
  • SETFIT with random sampling, the library's default.
  • SETFIT with hard negative/positive sampling, my variant.
  • SETFIT without fine-tuning ("No Fit"): a logistic regression on the pre-trained embeddings, to isolate how much step 1 contributes.

  • SETFIT vs. BERT with 18 examples per class: SETFIT was ahead by 22.6 points on average (the original paper reports 19.3). On SST-2, BERT stayed at 50.1%, which is chance level for two balanced classes, while SETFIT reached 89.1%.
  • With 50 examples per class the gap shrank to 13.3 points (5.6 in the original paper). BERT improves quickly with more data; SETFIT was already close to its ceiling.
  • Against BERT trained on 800 examples, SETFIT with 50 examples per class was 5.7 points behind on average. On SST-2, SETFIT with hard negative/positive sampling even beat it (93.2 vs. 92.1) with only 100 labeled examples.
  • Hard negative/positive sampling matched random sampling at M = 18 (68.4 vs. 68.6) and beat it by 1.1 points on average at M = 50, including +2.9 on SST-2. This fits the expectation that it helps once there are enough examples to find genuinely hard pairs.
  • Fine-tuning the Sentence Transformer matters: SETFIT beat the no-fine-tuning baseline on average at both sizes, by 4.3 points at M = 18. The exception is ADE-corpus-v2 at M = 50, where the pre-trained embeddings alone scored slightly higher (84.8 vs. 84.4), so with enough examples fine-tuning adds less on some datasets.
06 · Why It Works: Topic Embeddings

To see why SETFIT works, I encoded the SST-2 training and test sets with the pre-trained model and with the fine-tuned model, reduced the embeddings to two dimensions with t-SNE, and plotted them by class.

With the pre-trained embeddings, positive and negative reviews overlap heavily. After contrastive fine-tuning, the two classes form clearly separated clusters, on unseen test data as well as on the training data. Once the classes are nearly linearly separable, a simple logistic regression is enough to classify them well. That is why SETFIT can use such a lightweight head, and why step 1 accounts for most of the gain over the no-fine-tuning baseline.

07 · Limitations
  • SentEval-CR results are not meaningful. Every method, including BERT trained on 800 examples, scored below 50% on this balanced binary task, which is worse than guessing. These numbers point to a problem with that dataset's setup rather than a finding about any method, and they pull down every average in the table. The SST-2 and ADE-corpus-v2 results are where the comparison holds.
  • Extra cost for hard sampling: choosing hard pairs means computing the similarity of each sentence against every candidate in both classes, which takes longer than random sampling.
  • Scope: my implementation of hard negative/positive sampling covers binary classification only and is tied to cosine similarity. SETFIT itself supports multi-class tasks and other loss functions.
  • Scale: 3 datasets, 2 few-shot sizes, 5 seeds each, and no hyperparameter tuning. The +1.1 point gain is a promising signal, not a general result.
